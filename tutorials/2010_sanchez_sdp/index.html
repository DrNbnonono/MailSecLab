1. Problem & abstract

The paper sets out a sharp empirical question that was unanswered in 2010: to what degree can and do spammers forge the Received: trace information of spam emails?

Spammers have immense power and flexibility in forging email headers to mislead recipients and hide the true origin. Despite its critical importance for spam control and holding accountable the true originators of spam, there has been no systematic study on the forgery behavior of spammers. (Sanchez et al., CEAS 2010, §1)

The paper's contribution is twofold:

  1. Empirical measurement on a 3-year spam archive (≈ 1.84 M spam messages, 2007–2009) plus the MX records of ≈ 1.2 M network domains collected from FSU's inbound traffic.
  2. A reproducible algorithm for identifying the first external MTA (FEMTA) of an email — the only node whose trace data can be trusted, since it is inserted by the receiving domain.

2. Background: header fields & FEMTA

RFC 5322 only requires Date: and From:; any other header field can be freely faked by a sender. The two fields that cannot be trivially faked (because the receiving server inserts them) are:

  • Return-Path: — the SMTP MAIL FROM envelope address (added by the last MTA).
  • Received: — prepended by every MTA that handles the message. The clause from is filled from the TCP peer (and thus the from-address is trustworthy if the receiver is legitimate); the from-from hostname comes from the SMTP EHLO command and can be faked. The by-domain identifies the current MTA and is generally trustworthy.

The authors define the delivery path m₀ → m₁ → … → m_k and introduce two well-grounded, reusable definitions that all later MailSecLab experiments reuse:

  • FEMTA — the first node that does not belong to the recipient's network domain. Only the from-address inserted by the next (FIMTA) can be trusted.
  • FIMTA — the first internal MTA on the recipient side; its trace is the trust boundary for spam attribution.

4. Method — Algorithms 1 & 2, NLC paths

Algorithm 1 — Identifying the FEMTA

  1. Extract every Received: field hr_i.
  2. Derive the recipient domain d from Delivered-To:; if absent, fall back to the by-domain of the last Received:. (Never use the spoofable To:.)
  3. Resolve MX(d) to a set mx of in-domain mail servers.
  4. Walk Received: fields from hr_k backward to the first hr_i whose by-domain is in mx — this is the FIMTA.
  5. Continuing backward, stop at the first hr_j whose from-address is not in mx — that from-address is the FEMTA.

This correctness of this algorithm is anchored in handling the third-party MX case: e.g., the bait domain untroubled.org is served by mx.futurequest.net, so the naïve heuristic of "first node whose from-domain ≠ recipient-domain" would mis-identify the FEMTA.

Algorithm 2 — Determining the registered domain of a host name

Used when clustering mail servers by domain in the MX dataset:

  • If the TLD length > 2 (e.g., .edu), the registered domain is the last two labels.
  • If TLD length = 2 (e.g., .cn) and the SLD matches ac|co|gv|or|tm|com|edu|gov|nom|org|asso|gouv|info|priv|sport, the registered domain is the last three labels; otherwise two.
  • Exception: hosts under dyndns.biz always take three labels.

The heuristic is intentionally conservative: it may split a single organization into multiple clusters, but it never merges two unrelated organizations. MailSecLab uses the same conservative bias when collapsing alternates like .com.cn ↔ .cn.

Network-Level Consistent (NLC) paths

For a path m₀ … m_k with fi = from-domain of hr_i and bi = by-domain of hr_i, the i-th hop is NLC if fi and b_{i-1} share a /16 (or, where IP is not resolvable, the same registered domain). The NLC path is the maximal prefix where every adjacent hop is NLC; the first non-NLC hop is treated as the spammer's insertion.

5.1 Length — short and shrinking

The headline interpretation: botnets and direct-from-MUA sending became the dominant pattern, so most spam arrives at the FIMTA with only one upstream hop. Spammers appear to have largely abandoned elaborate Received-header forgery, and the simple NLC test exposes most of the remaining forgeries (jumps to the wrong /16, swapped from-domain ↔ by-domain).

5.2 Network-level distribution

Plotting spam volume and distinct FEMTA IP counts per /16 prefix shows three concentrated address-space clusters; their locations are consistent across years and consistent with prior work (Duan et al., ICC 2007), which cross-validates the representativeness of the spam archive.

6. Findings on MX records

  • 57 % of the 1.2 M domains have exactly one mail server in their MX record (no backup relay at all).
  • 90 % of domains have all their MX targets in one mail-server cluster (single registered domain); 99 % have ≤ 2 clusters. Multiple MX records in the same domain are normally load-balancing, not relay chains.
  • The largest outlier is zartana.com with 94 mail servers; the largest cluster count is 7 (one MX record per cluster).
  • Top mail-server local-name prefixes in the MX dataset (966,397 hostnames): mail 34.2 %, mail2 2.4 %, mx 2.1 %, smtp 1.8 %, mx1 1.5 %.

For FEMTAs in the spam archive the naming distribution is strikingly different (183,296 hostnames): the top three are IP-as-hostname patterns (a-b-c-d 16.8 %, xyz-a-b-c-d 13.7 %, a.b.c.d 11.1 %) — i.e. ≈ 41.5 % of FEMTAs are residential/dial-up machines identifiable by their hostname shape, and ≈ 45.5 % have no reverse DNS at all.

7. Implications on authentication & filtering

7.1 Email sender authentication

  • Because typical mail traverses only two domains, the trust boundary is usually recoverable from FEMTA + FIMTA alone — DKIM/signing schemes are valuable when path length matters, but mail-server-level (MSLA) schemes like SPF capture most of the residual risk, because botnet machines cannot easily impersonate a legitimate mail server IP.
  • If mailing lists and .forward-like relays were to verify the sender domain before accepting a message, that already closes most attribution gaps on the two-domain path.

7.2 Spam filtering

  • Reverse-DNS lookup of FEMTAs at TCP-accept time, combined with a community convention that real mail servers use mail.*/mx.* hostnames, can drop a large fraction of botnet traffic without global DNSBL probing.
  • Trace-record length itself becomes a feature: very short paths with mismatched by-domain/from-domain are a strong signal of botnet sending.
  • Open relays still occur but are increasingly hostile to spammers — useful mostly for hiding topology, not for delivery volume.

8. Limitations & future work

  • Spam corpus is from a single vantage point (10 bait domains); the representativeness has only been cross-validated against one other study.
  • Only spam is examined — no path-length comparison to legitimate mail.
  • Forgery detection is conservative by design (deliberately biased toward under-counting forged nodes), so reported forgery rates are lower bounds.
  • Algorithm 1 is heuristic; it fails when the receiving domain hides all MX behind a CDN with no published record.

9. How MailSecLab uses this paper

  • H-series (Received authenticity). Sanchez 2010 is the conceptual parent of H1/H2/H3 — accepting that botnets dominate the upstream of every modern spam trace and that the relevant trust boundary is FEMTA → FIMTA. Our H/I generator uses Algorithm 1 verbatim as the FEMTA oracle.
  • I2 (self-consistent forged Received chain). Sanity floor for a "good forgery": NLC + FEMTA-shaped upstream + mail/mx/smtp hostname. If the synthetic chain fails any of those, the chain is exposed as fake by the same filter that exposes real botnet spam in 2010.
  • FEMTA heuristics as label source. When we label real spam corpora for DKIM / Received experiments, Algorithm 1 is the FEMTA extractor; we add a conservative check that the FEMTA IP belongs to the same /16 as the claimed from-address for the same reasons the paper uses /16 NLC.
  • Naming baseline. The 966,397-host MX hostname distribution (top local names) is the reference distribution we compare new MX-clusters against when studying forwarding chains (Liu 2023) and phishing infrastructure (Luo 2025).
