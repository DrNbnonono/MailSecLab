1. Core thesis — chain of weak links

The authenticity of an email depends on the weakest link in the authentication chain. Even a harmless issue may cause unprecedented damages when it is integrated into a more extensive system. (Shen et al., USENIX 2021, §1)

The authors frame email "authenticity" as a chain distributed across four roles and multiple independent protocols:

  • Protocols: SPF (RFC 7208), DKIM (RFC 6376), DMARC (RFC 7489), plus the experimental ARC (RFC 8617).
  • Roles: sender MUA, sender MTA, receiver MTA, forwarder MTA, receiver MUA ("Email UI Rendering").
  • Failure modes: weak links among protocols (their grammatical edges leave ambiguity that different actors interpret differently), weak links among roles (no contract says the forwarder must verify the sender), and weak links among services (every provider wires the protocols with different defaults).

3. Catalogue of 14 attacks (A1–A14)

Sending-stage

  • A1 — Auth username ≠ MAIL FROM. Self-hosted Zimbra / EwoMail allow any combination; attacker can send as alice@a.com while the AUTH login is oscar@a.com. Zoho, 139.com block it; the others did not at measurement time.
  • A2 — MAIL FROM ≠ From. Many webmail providers and 14 of 30 tested services don't restrict this; the recipient MUA only shows From so no warning fires.

Receiver-stage (parser / protocol gaps)

  • A3 — Empty MAIL FROM. RFC 5321 allows an empty MAIL FROM (bounce-avoidance). 13 services (Yahoo, Yeah, 126, AOL …) treat this as SPF "none" instead of "fail"; the spoofed From then sails through DMARC.
  • A4 — Multiple From headers. 19 services accept. The MUA displays the first From; the MTA may use a different (attacker-controlled) From for DMARC alignment → misalignment becomes alignment.
  • A5 — Multiple addresses in one From. RFC 5322 allows it; 15 services accept and 6 even show all of them.
  • A6 — Parsing-inconsistency attacks:
    • Route portion <@a.com,@b.com:admin@c.com>.
    • Null mailbox-list <a@a.com> ,<b@b.com>.
    • Comment trickery <admin(username)@a.com(domain name)>.
    • Truncated parsers — NUL (\x00), invisible Unicode (＀–￿, \x81–\xff), or semantic chars ([ ] { } , ; : \t \r \n) cause the MTA to see a different domain than the MUA renders.
  • A7 — MIME-encoded address. From: =?utf-8?b?…?= makes DMARC extractor return "none" while the MUA shows the decoded address Alice@a.com. 7 services impacted incl. Outlook and Office 365.
  • A8 — Subdomain attack. From: admin@mail.google.com has no SPF; the parent google.com's strict policy doesn't help when DMARC policy is checked at the subdomain. 13 services impacted.

Forwarder-stage

  • A9 — Open forwarding. 12 services let an account forward to any address with no recipient-side confirmation. Attackers register oscar@a.com, configure forwarding to bob@b.com, and bypass trust assumptions.
  • A10 — DKIM Signature Fraud. Forwarder (a.com) naively re-signs every forwarded message with d=a.com. An attacker that registers oscar@a.com, configures forward to oscar@c.com, sends a spoofed From=Alice@a.com → the From's DKIM is signed by a.com → passes DMARC. Alibaba Cloud, Office 365, Yahoo confirmed vulnerable.
  • A11 — ARC bugs. Office 365 and Zoho produce or consume ARC Authentication Results (AAR) incorrectly. Spoofed mail that Zoho auto-forwards via Gmail shows a spurious DMARC pass in AAR, breaking the ARC trust chain.

UI-rendering-stage

  • A12 — IDN homograph. paypal.com vs. xn–aypal-uye.com rendered identically when the address bar applies no script detection. iCloud and Gmail render the latter as "From: admin@paypal.com". 10 services affected.
  • A13 — Missing UI rendering. Invisible characters (U+0000–U+001F, U+FF00–U+FFFF) and semantic characters (@ : ; ") silently truncate or remove parts of the From header. 12 services affected incl. Zoho, 163, Sohu.
  • A14 — Right-to-left override. U+202E moc.a@‭alice displays as Alice@a.com. 11 services affected incl. Outlook, Yahoo, Yandex.

Only 12 / 30 services perform Sender Inconsistency Checks (SIC) at the UI layer. Many clients also lack SIC. All 30 vendors deployed SPF/DKIM/DMARC validation at the receiving MTA, but most still allow at least one bypass.

5. Cocktail attacks & real impact

Single-stage attacks have a finite ceiling; combining two attacks at different stages makes the spoofing email look entirely legitimate. The paper's two canonical examples:

Case 1 — Yahoo + iCloud + A2 + A4 (shared MTA model)

  1. Attacker logs into Yahoo with their Yahoo account.
  2. First From: oscar@yahoo.com passes Yahoo's same-domain check.
  3. Second From: admin@paypal.com is appended; iCloud doesn't reject multi-From.
  4. iCloud uses first From for DMARC alignment (yahoo.com) and displays second From (admin@paypal.com) to the user.
  5. Net effect: SPF=pass, DKIM=pass, DMARC=pass (yahoo alignment), UI shows "From: admin@paypal.com", no SIC alert.

Case 2 — Alibaba + Gmail + A2+A3+A10 (direct + forward MTA)

  1. Attacker sends via direct MTA a message with empty MAIL FROM and From=admin@aliyun.com to their own Alibaba Cloud Mail account (oscar@aliyun.com) → SPF fails (empty MAIL FROM), DKIM fails.
  2. Alibaba Cloud accepts the message (no outbound FROM check) and forwards per attacker's rule to oscar@attack.com — re-signs with d=aliyun.com.
  3. Attacker re-sends the spoofed email with MAIL FROM:admin@attack.com directly to Gmail.
  4. Gmail: SPF verifies attack.com (pass), DKIM verifies aliyun.com (pass), DMARC alignment is satisfied because the original From=admin@aliyun.com aligns with DKIM. No UI warning.

The paper received positive responses from 11 vendors including Gmail, Yahoo, iCloud, Alibaba Cloud, Coremail. Sina paid ≈ $90; Yandex paid $200; Microsoft assigned severity "Important" and paid a bounty; Zoho shipped the paper's UI notification scheme to production.

6. Root causes

  1. Weak links among multi-protocols: SPF/DKIM/DMARC each cover a subset; if any link fails the chain breaks. Multiple From headers and MIME encoding exploit the fact that no single specification author owns the cross-protocol grammar.
  2. Weak links among multi-roles: forwarders and UI renderers have no standardized responsibility for verifying sender authenticity — they inherit whatever the previous hop did.
  3. Weak links among multi-services: every provider wires SPF/DKIM/ ARC slightly differently, creating "trust but incompatible" interfaces. Big providers tolerate bad mail to keep interop; small providers are under-resourced to handle all RFC edge cases.

7. Mitigation & disclosure

  • More accurate RFC specifications — e.g. DKIM should specify when a forwarder is allowed to attach a new signature (never on mail that did not verify at the upstream).
  • UI Notification scheme — surface the inconsistency between AUTH username, MAIL FROM, From, and DKIM d= in the MUA. Coremail adopted the paper's recommended scheme; a Chrome extension NoSpoofing implements it for Gmail.
  • Evaluation tool — an open-source attacker-side fuzzer at github.com/mo-xiaoxi/Email-SpoofingTestTool for self-audit.
  • Standardize the four roles: especially forwarder responsibility and minimum UI-side checks.

8. How MailSecLab uses this paper

  • K-series (DKIM verifier differential): A6 (parsing-inconsistency), A7 (MIME-encoded), and A11 (ARC errors) are the standard paradigms we stress-test across Postfix, Exim, OpenSMTPD, opendkim, perl-mail-dkim, and rspamd. The paper's finding that "all 30 vendors are vulnerable to ≥ 1 attack" sets our pass bar.
  • F-series (parser differential across mail utilities): A6 is the reason F1 generates \x00, ￿, and {\r,\n,\t} probes — these are exactly the categories named in §4.2.
  • I-series (synthetic trace & forwarding experiments): A9 + A10 are the minimum viable templates for "realistic spoofed mail that passes DKIM+DMARC" — used as ground truth for I-series label quality.
  • H-series (Received trust boundary): A2 and A4 motivate why H2 must verify the last Received: hop (FIMTA) and never trust by-domain values without corroborating from-address from the next hop.
  • Disclosure harness: MailSecLab runs an internal alert test using the paper's "spoofing success = delivered to inbox + no SIC" definition, exactly as the paper formalizes it in §3.4.
