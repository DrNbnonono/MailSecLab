1. Problem & contributions

Phishing attacks on enterprise employees present one of the most costly and potent threats to organizations. We explore an under-studied facet of enterprise phishing attacks: the email relay infrastructure behind successfully delivered phishing emails. (Luo et al., 2025, Abstract)

Two decades after Sanchez/Duan/Dong's botnet-era study, this paper asks the same network-origins question on a much larger and more relevant corpus: emails actually delivered into enterprise inboxes. Key contributions:

  • Collaboration with Barracuda Networks to analyze the full set of SMTP headers for one year across thousands of organizations on Office 365.
  • Characterizes phishing by source network rather than by content, and introduces a taxonomy of "phishing-concentration" by ASN.
  • Deploys the conclusions as a real-time classifier in Barracuda's production pipeline, retrieving a 3–5 % daily recall uplift over existing detectors with zero false positives during the deployment period.

All customers use Microsoft Office 365; data is opt-in, IRB-approved, and stored encrypted. Importantly, the corpus only contains emails that passed Barracuda's pre-filter, so it represents what an enterprise inbox actually sees.

FEMTA extraction

  1. Resolve the recipient's MX from the historical DNS snapshot at delivery time.
  2. Walk the Received: headers from the bottom up; the first hop whose by-domain matches the MX is the FIMTA.
  3. The very next hop (the upstream of FIMTA) is the FEMTA — its IP and domain are reliably stamped by FIMTA.
  4. Drop Received: headers whose IP is in a private/reserved range before selecting the originating IP (the first hop whose IP is public and non-reserved).
  5. Map each IP to ASN using Team Cymru's IP-to-ASN service.

3. Forgery headroom (Received-source fidelity)

Before trusting the FEMTA label, Luo et al. ran four tests that flag probable Received:-header tampering on a phishing email:

  1. 66 % of phishing emails: MX-identified sender IP and the purported origin IP belong to the same AS → robust at AS level regardless of tampering.
  2. Add +16.2 %: the same sender-IP / origin-IP pair has been observed in a clean corpus from the same window.
  3. Add +2 %: the entire relay path IP list exactly matches a path in clean corpus (account takeover pattern).
  4. Add +6.4 %: the recipient MX appears exactly once in Received: and either equals the origin IP or shares its AS (an single-AS sender pattern).

Combined, ≥ 90.6 % of phishing emails in the dataset do not show evidence of tampered source IPs in ways that would distort AS-level analysis. The remaining 9.4 % have path-length distributions identical to clean mail — i.e. no detectable forgery. This is the modern analogue of Sanchez 2010's "botnet-direct-to-MX" finding: when email reaches Barracuda customers, Received: forgery is rare and only minor.

4. Findings — IP- and AS-level distributions

Source-IP concentration

The top 200 source IPs send ≈ 50 % of all phishing emails but < 1 % of clean email. This is the inverse of the spam-era pattern from Ramachandran & Feamster (SIGCOMM 2006), where the same set of IPs sent most clean mail — for phishing, the relevant IPs are nearly disjoint from the legitimate high-volume senders.

Source-AS concentration

Top 100 ASes by phishing volume:

  • Carry ≈ 80 % of phishing email and ≈ 70 % of clean email.
  • The top 3 ASes alone send ≈ 60 % of all legitimate email — all three belong to Amazon (AS 14618, AS 16509) and Microsoft (AS 8075).
  • Excluding Amazon and Microsoft, the top-100 by phishing volume drop to ~ < 10 % of clean email. Hence: excluding AWS / Azure, network-based reputation is highly informative.

5. Phishing-concentration taxonomy

The defining per-AS metric is the "phishing probability" #phishing / #(phishing + clean) sent by that AS. Three buckets:

By tier share of all phishing email: Low ≈ 35.6 %, Medium ≈ 19.7 %, High ≈ 39.7 %. Time-stability differs sharply:

  • The top-100 ASes by phishing volume are stable: 31 ASes persist across all three months and contribute ≈ 44–51 % of monthly phishing volume. 26 of those 31 are hosting providers.
  • The "high-concentration" label is transient: only 9 ASes stay "high" across all three months; most enter or leave the bucket between months — a signal that attackers burn ASNs after compromising them.

6. Geographic routes

  • Phishing emails traverse slightly more countries in their Received: chain than clean mail; the bulk of both still concentrate in US-domestic paths.
  • The top-25 country-routes account for > 60 % of phishing traffic. Country-level features alone are weaker than AS-level features, but still useful (the top 5 high-phishing-probability countries listed in the paper: Japan, Vietnam, Greece, Moldova, Croatia).
  • Email authentication (SPF/DKIM/DMARC) pass rates are themselves weak signals — only 10.4 % of phishing emails pass DMARC and only 30 % of clean emails do. Authentication headers are the least important features in the classifier.

7. Production classifier (3–5 % recall gain)

Luo et al. deploy an online classifier at Barracuda that uses only two features (per-day, per-IP):

  1. Phishing volume — number of phishing emails sent from the IP that day.
  2. Phishing probability — ratio of phishing to clean from the IP in a sliding window.

The sliding window n was tuned offline (best at n > 0 days, finer-grained beyond 30-minute micro-bursts proved noisy). Over a ~5-month deployment window:

Over a span of nearly 5 months, the new detector increased the number of emails flagged as phishing by 3–5 % per day (beyond those detected by Barracuda's existing detectors), with no false positives based on manual inspection. (§6, Luo et al., 2025)

Manual sampling of one day/week confirmed that newly-flagged emails were not only phishing but also BEC / scam / spam that existing detectors had missed.

8. Limitations

  • Dataset is dominated by US/Europe enterprises on Office 365 → may not reflect phishing infrastructure seen by Asian or Latin American organizations.
  • Barracuda's pre-filters have already removed the most obvious phishing before the corpus is captured — the paper studies "successful" phishing, not the bulk arrival rate.
  • Up to early 2021 corpus; static blocklists and cloud-provider abuse mitigations have shifted since.
  • Few phishing emails come from Gmail/Outlook published IP ranges (rate limits and outbound filtering makes user-account compromise harder to weaponize at scale) — a structural blind spot in this study that nonetheless matches the K-series DKIM work in MailSecLab.

9. How MailSecLab uses this paper

  • H-series: confirms Sanchez 2010's "FEMTA is trustworthy" claim on a corpus 1000× larger. MailSecLab's H1/H2 use Algorithm 1 verbatim and validate it against Barracuda's femta labels. The 90.6 % non-forgery ceiling in this paper is our upper bound for "honest" Received chain reconstruction in I2.
  • I2 (forged Received chains): any synthetic chain must be consistent with the AS-class prior — the top-100 phishing ASes, plus the 9 stable "high-concentration" ASes, are the realistic forging targets.
  • K-series (DKIM verifier differential): 30 % clean DMARC pass rate and 10.4 % phishing DMARC pass rate quantify the difficulty that DKIM-only experiments face. Authentication headers are too weak to be a label.
  • Outlook / O365 bias awareness: the entire dataset is O365 mailboxes; any generalization to other providers should be checked against this caveat.
